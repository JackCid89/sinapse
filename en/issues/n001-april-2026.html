Cover

India · Energy · Nuclear engineering

22 years later: India's Kalpakkam fast breeder reactor reaches criticality

On April 6, 2026 at 20:25, the 500 MWe PFBR began a controlled fission chain. India joins the exclusive club of commercial fast-reactor operators — but the road ahead is longer than the one already walked.

By the SINAPSE editorial team · April 28, 2026

What happened that night in Kokkilamedu, on the Tamil Nadu coast, was technically modest: a reactor brought to minimum power, enough to sustain a chain reaction but not yet to produce a useful kilowatt. What happened institutionally was the opposite of modest. India had spent two decades trying to close a promise made in the 1970s, when Homi Bhabha argued that the country — uranium-poor but thorium-rich — had to build its own nuclear path. The Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR), second step of a three-stage plan, is that path turned into steel, liquid sodium, and MOX fuel.

A fast breeder reactor does exactly what the name says: it produces more fissile fuel than it consumes. The PFBR core runs on a mixed uranium-plutonium oxide, surrounded by a blanket of uranium-238. Fast neutrons — unmoderated, unlike conventional pressurized water reactors — transmute that U-238 into fissile plutonium-239. The promise is geopolitical before it is technical: a country without large uranium reserves can multiply its fuel inventory if it manages to recycle the blanket's plutonium and, eventually, transmute thorium-232 into uranium-233. That is the third stage of the Bhabha plan, still in the future.

The arithmetic of time

Building the PFBR took longer than expected by almost any metric. Ground was broken in October 2004, with a criticality target of 2010. The schedule slipped nine times. The causes pile up: seismic criteria rewritten after Fukushima in 2011, a full redesign of the sodium-water heat exchanger, problems with primary sodium pumps, a chronic shortage of qualified MOX fuel, and the hard — expensive — lesson of choosing to build before fully prototyping at scale. India had been operating an experimental fast reactor, the FBTR (40 MWt), since 1985; but jumping to 500 MWe was a different order of complexity.

Liquid sodium is the recurring villain of every fast-reactor project. It reacts violently with water, ignites in air, and becomes radioactive in a neutron flux. The industry carries a record of incidents: Japan's Monju never recovered from its 1995 sodium fire; France's Superphénix closed in 1998 after a decade of trouble; Russia's BN-600 has logged more than a dozen leaks over its lifetime. Against that backdrop, reaching first criticality with no reportable incident is, in itself, an engineering achievement.

What gets tested now

First criticality is not electricity generation. What comes next is months of stepped power ascent: from zero-power to a few megawatts thermal to verify reactivity coefficients, then to 30%, 70%, and 100%. Grid connection is scheduled by the Department of Atomic Energy for late 2026 or 2027 if the test phase finds no surprises. Each stage requires verifying that sodium flows as the safety codes predict, that control rods drop within the expected window, and that leak detectors distinguish signal from noise.

"First criticality proves the design works; grid connection would prove the economics work; fuel recycling would prove the promise works. We're at step one."

The economic system isn't closed either. The PFBR's final cost will comfortably exceed 100 billion rupees against an initial budget of 35 billion. For the thorium cycle to close, India also needs to commission the Fast Reactor Fuel Cycle Facility (FRFCF), also at Kalpakkam, still under construction. Without reprocessing, the bred fuel sits as high-level waste.

Why it matters outside India

Fast reactors are back on the agenda in countries like the United States — where TerraPower and Oklo pursue distinct designs — the United Kingdom, France, and South Korea. The appeal is multiple: they can burn down accumulated civilian plutonium, reduce the volume and lifetime of waste, and promise uranium utilization between 60 and 100 times higher than a PWR. But no Western country operates a commercial fast reactor today. If the PFBR sustains nominal output over the next two years, it will deliver the first contemporary empirical data — outside Russia and China — on the economics of operating one.

What isn't said in the official communiqués is the other face: fast reactors produce plutonium of high isotopic quality, technically suitable for weapons use. India, which never signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty, keeps the PFBR outside IAEA safeguards. That isn't new — it always was — but it makes criticality an event watched through a double lens in Islamabad, Washington, and Beijing.

What's missing and what stays open

Three questions matter today. First: how long until 100% power, and what is the real capacity factor in year one? Earlier fast reactors didn't pass 30–60%. Second: when does the FRFCF facility, without which there is no closed cycle, come online? Third — the one no one in India answers in writing: is this reactor the first of a series of six promised in earlier plans, or the last of its kind, superseded by newer designs? What we learn over the next two years will decide whether the April 6 criticality marks the start of a new nuclear era or the prolonged closing of an old one.

§ 03

Papers

Four recent publications with summary, authorship, and why they matter.

AstronomyExoplanet atmospheres

Direct imaging of Epsilon Indi Ab reveals water-ice clouds and an ammonia shortfall

Matthews et al. · Max Planck Institute for Astronomy · Nature · April 2026

The team combined JWST/MIRI and NIRSpec observations of Epsilon Indi Ab, a young Jupiter analog (~3 MJ) at 3.6 pc from the Sun. Low-resolution spectroscopy shows H₂O absorption consistent with ice clouds, while ammonia expected from thermochemical equilibrium is underrepresented.

Why it matters: cold, directly imageable atmospheres are a critical lab for validating gas-giant models. If the NH₃ deficit is confirmed, vertical-transport and disequilibrium-chemistry codes assumed for brown dwarfs and temperate exoplanets need revision.

CosmologyAsiaN-body simulation

HyperMillenium: the highest-resolution cosmological simulation to date

International team led by the Chinese Academy of Sciences · ChinaXiv / Science Advances · April 2026

Hydrodynamic N-body simulation with ~3 × 10¹³ particles in a 3 Gpc/h volume, capable of resolving dwarf-galaxy mass halos in a large-scale cosmological context. The first batch of results addresses massive-cluster formation at z > 4 and subhalo statistics.

Why it matters: for the first time a single simulation covers both cluster and dwarf-galaxy regimes in the same volume without requiring separate zoom-ins. Exactly the kind of testbed Euclid and the next generation of optical surveys need.

Quantum computingError correction

A QEC architecture that lowers the threshold to ~10,000 physical qubits

Caltech / Oratomic team · arXiv preprint · April 2026

Proposed error-correction code combining concatenation with continuous bosonic encoding and optimized magic-state distillation routes. The theoretical projection: a fault-tolerant quantum computer useful for RSA-2048 factoring would need 10,000–20,000 physical qubits, instead of the millions estimated before.

Why it matters: if the result holds up under peer review, it compresses the hardware barrier by 50–100×. It changes the timeline for post-quantum cryptography and pushes the migration to NIST-PQC standards on critical infrastructure up the priority list.

PaleontologyAsiaEvolution

A late-Ediacaran transitional fauna in Yunnan bridges the gap toward modern phyla

Yunnan University and Virginia Tech team · Science · April 2, 2026

The Wenghui biota site documents bilaterally symmetric organisms with evidence of complete digestive tubes before the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary. U-Pb dating places the formation between 551 and 543 Ma. The assemblage includes at least four previously undescribed morphotypes.

Why it matters: helps disentangle whether the "Cambrian explosion" was as abrupt as the fossil record suggested, or whether basal body plans appeared tens of millions of years earlier and the record is incomplete. Implications for molecular clocks.

§ 06

Column

The reactor that took 22 years: why energy infrastructure doesn't accelerate

By Jack Cid

A question keeps coming up every time a very long technical project finally crosses a line: what does "late" mean? The Kalpakkam PFBR took 22 years to reach first criticality. The common reading is management failure — sixteen years behind a six-year schedule. But that reading assumes the original schedule was a reasonable prediction. It almost never was.

The software industry lives the opposite illusion. A new feature in GPT-5.5 ships six weeks after GPT-5.4 because the same hardware runs the next version without changing anything physical. The bit doesn't rust, the transformer doesn't need certification, there is no liquid sodium at 550 °C in a 30-meter pipe. Physical infrastructure — reactors, electric grids, railways, canals, factories — moves on a different scale because it obeys a different law: the law of systems that can only fail expensively, and to which we therefore demand margins software never knew.

Looking at the PFBR alongside DeepSeek V4 this same week is an uncomfortable, useful exercise. Both are at the frontier, both come from the Global South, both compete against richer countries. But their metric of progress is radically different. DeepSeek can iterate ten versions a year; Kalpakkam iterates once a generation. When public discourse compares industries — "if AI moves this fast, why doesn't energy?" — it usually misses that the answer isn't laziness or bureaucracy. It's the asymmetry of how much it costs to fix a mistake.

A software mistake is patched with a commit. A mistake in a fast reactor is paid for, in the worst case, with the decline of an entire technology line. Monju proved it: a single sodium fire in 1995 erased three decades of Japan's fast-reactor program. France's Superphénix never recovered the public trust after the decade of its operations. Industrial economics punishes physical error along a far steeper curve than digital economics does. People who design reactors learn to operate under that constraint and therefore to move slowly. That isn't a defect. It's the system working as it should.

That doesn't mean there are no decisions to question. India's choice to jump from a 40 MWt experimental reactor to a 500 MWe commercial one with no intermediate stage is debatable — and probably an error. The choice to make everything indigenously, including sodium pumps designed in-house when foreign suppliers had decades of experience, is too. But those are political and strategic decisions, not engineering failures. They point to the other layer: whether the price of autonomy is worth paying when importing would have been faster.

The connection worth underlining this week is this: in the coming years we will hear comparisons between the "speed of AI" and the "slowness of energy." That narrative should be resisted. AI can accelerate because its errors are cheap. Energy can't, not because it lacks ambition but because its errors are catastrophic. When the two intersect — and they're already intersecting, in data centers that need 2028 SMRs that probably won't exist — the faster system has to learn the cadence of the slower one, not the other way around.

Twenty-two years sounds long. On the scale of an energy infrastructure built to last 60, it's not. The question isn't why it took so long. It's whether the next 60 will be worth the wait.

Direct spectroscopy of a young gas giant (~3 MJ) at 3.6 pc. Detection of H₂O absorption consistent with ice clouds; NH₃ deficit relative to chemical equilibrium.
