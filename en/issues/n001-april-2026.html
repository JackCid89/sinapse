§ 02

Cover

India · Energy · Nuclear engineering

22 years later: India's Kalpakkam fast breeder reactor reaches criticality

On April 6, 2026 at 20:25, the 500 MWe PFBR began a controlled fission chain. India joins the exclusive club of commercial fast-reactor operators — but the road ahead is longer than the one already walked.

By the SINAPSE editorial teamApril 28, 2026

What happened that night in Kokkilamedu, on the Tamil Nadu coast, was technically modest: a reactor brought to minimum power, enough to sustain a chain reaction but not yet to produce a useful kilowatt. What happened institutionally was the opposite of modest. India had spent two decades trying to close a promise made in the 1970s, when Homi Bhabha argued that the country — uranium-poor but thorium-rich — had to build its own nuclear path.

A fast breeder reactor does what its name says: it produces more fissile fuel than it consumes. The PFBR core runs on a mixed uranium-plutonium oxide, surrounded by a blanket of uranium-238. Fast neutrons — unmoderated — transmute that U-238 into fissile plutonium-239.

The arithmetic of time

Building the PFBR took longer than expected by almost any metric. Ground was broken in October 2004, with a criticality target of 2010. The schedule slipped nine times. Causes accumulate: seismic criteria rewritten after Fukushima, redesign of the sodium-water heat exchanger, chronic shortage of qualified MOX fuel.

Liquid sodium is the recurring villain. Japan's Monju never recovered from its 1995 sodium fire; France's Superphénix closed in 1998; Russia's BN-600 has logged more than a dozen leaks. Reaching first criticality with no reportable incident is, in itself, an engineering achievement.

What gets tested now

First criticality is not electricity generation. Months of stepped power ascent follow: 30 %, 70 %, 100 %. Grid connection is scheduled for late 2026 or 2027. The final cost will comfortably exceed 100 billion rupees against an initial budget of 35 billion.

What isn't said in official communiqués: fast reactors produce plutonium of high isotopic quality, suitable for weapons use. India, which never signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty, keeps the PFBR outside IAEA safeguards.

Three open questions

First: how long until 100 % power, and what is the real capacity factor in year one? Earlier fast reactors didn't pass 30-60 %. Second: when does the FRFCF reprocessing facility come online? Third: is this the first of a series of six promised in earlier plans, or the last of its kind?

§ 03

Papers

Four recent publications with summary, authorship, and why they matter.

AstronomyExoplanet atmospheres

Direct imaging of Epsilon Indi Ab reveals water-ice clouds and an ammonia shortfall

Matthews et al. · Max Planck Institute for Astronomy · Nature · April 2026

Direct spectroscopy of a young Jupiter analog (~3 MJ) at 3.6 pc. H₂O absorption consistent with ice clouds; NH₃ deficit relative to chemical equilibrium.

Cold, directly imageable atmospheres are a critical lab for validating gas-giant models. If the NH₃ deficit is confirmed, vertical-transport and disequilibrium-chemistry codes need revision.

CosmologyAsiaN-body simulation

HyperMillenium: the highest-resolution cosmological simulation to date

International team led by the Chinese Academy of Sciences · ChinaXiv / Science Advances · April 2026

Hydrodynamic N-body simulation with ~3 × 10¹³ particles in a 3 Gpc/h volume, resolving dwarf-galaxy mass halos.

For the first time a single simulation covers both cluster and dwarf-galaxy regimes in the same volume. Testbed for Euclid and the next generation of optical surveys.

Quantum computingError correction

A QEC architecture that lowers the threshold to ~10,000 physical qubits

Caltech / Oratomic team · arXiv preprint · April 2026

Error-correction code combining concatenation with continuous bosonic encoding. Theoretical projection: 10,000-20,000 physical qubits for RSA-2048 factoring, instead of millions.

If the result holds up under peer review, it compresses the hardware barrier by 50-100×. Changes the timeline for post-quantum cryptography.

PaleontologyAsiaEvolution

A late-Ediacaran transitional fauna in Yunnan bridges the gap toward modern phyla

Yunnan University and Virginia Tech · Science · April 2, 2026

The Wenghui biota site documents bilaterally symmetric organisms with complete digestive tubes between 551 and 543 Ma. At least four previously undescribed morphotypes.

Helps disentangle whether the "Cambrian explosion" was as abrupt as the fossil record suggested, or whether basal body plans appeared tens of millions of years earlier.

§ 06

Column

The reactor that took 22 years: why energy infrastructure doesn't accelerate

By Jack CidApril 28, 2026

A question keeps coming up every time a very long technical project finally crosses a line: what does "late" mean? The Kalpakkam PFBR took 22 years to reach first criticality. The common reading is management failure — sixteen years behind a six-year schedule. But that reading assumes the original schedule was a reasonable prediction. It almost never was.

The software industry lives the opposite illusion. A new feature in GPT-5.5 ships six weeks after GPT-5.4 because the same hardware runs the next version without changing anything physical. The bit doesn't rust, the transformer doesn't need certification, there is no liquid sodium at 550 °C in a 30-meter pipe. Physical infrastructure moves on a different scale because it obeys a different law.

Looking at the PFBR alongside DeepSeek V4 this same week is uncomfortable and useful. Both are at the frontier, both come from the Global South, both compete against richer countries. But their metric of progress is radically different. DeepSeek can iterate ten versions a year; Kalpakkam iterates once a generation.

A software mistake is patched with a commit. A mistake in a fast reactor is paid for, in the worst case, with the decline of an entire technology line. Monju proved it: a single sodium fire in 1995 erased three decades of Japan's fast-reactor program. Industrial economics punishes physical error along a far steeper curve than digital economics does.

In the coming years we will hear comparisons between the "speed of AI" and the "slowness of energy." That narrative should be resisted. AI can accelerate because its errors are cheap. Energy can't, not because it lacks ambition but because its errors are catastrophic. When the two intersect — in data centers that need 2028 SMRs that probably won't exist — the faster system has to learn the cadence of the slower one, not the other way around.

Twenty-two years sounds long. On the scale of an energy infrastructure built to last 60, it's not. The question isn't why it took so long. It's whether the next 60 will be worth the wait.

§ 07

Counterpoint

AI datacenters: what really had to be paid, and who is actually paying?

By Passiflora CaeruleaApril 28, 2026

«AI is going to break the power grid» / «Datacenters don't consume that much, let's not exaggerate». Stock phrases of the debate, both sides, 2024–2026

The public debate over AI datacenters has found two ways to be wrong at the same time. Catastrophism: AI will collapse the world's power grid. Dismissal: it's only 2 % of global consumption. Both argue with global averages about a phenomenon whose problem is local and distributional.

Atlas of the problem · 7 dimensions
Countries in the headline
USA (Virginia, Texas, Arizona), Ireland (Dublin), China (Hebei, Guizhou), Singapore.
Countries in the actual story
Chile (66 projects in 2026, up from 6 in 2017; Google halted Cerrillos over water demand), Mexico (279 MW operational, protests in Querétaro and Colón), Brazil (37 % of LATAM capacity), Uruguay, Paraguay, Kenya, South Africa, Malaysia (Johor displaced Singapore). LATAM capacity grows >30 % per year.
Who profits
NVIDIA: ~75 % gross margin on H100/Blackwell; captures ~70 % of stack value. Hyperscalers: 60-70 % operating margin. Industrial real estate: 10-15 % yield. Host governments: regimes that reduce taxes by 40-90 %.
Who pays
Residential tariffs: Northern Virginia +15-20 % since 2022. Water: Cerrillos was to consume 7 billion liters/year (equivalent to 80,000 people). Real estate: -3 to -7 % in perimeter. Scope 3 emissions from hardware fabricated in Asia, typically omitted.
5 / 10-year horizon
2030: 3-4 % of world electricity (IEA, 650-1,050 TWh). Bottleneck migrates from chip to substation. 2035: if SMRs don't land, hyperscalers buy geothermal (Kenya, Iceland), hydro (Paraguay, DRC), solar (Chile, Morocco). If efficient models win, total demand moderates 2-3×.
Regulatory gap
EU: AI Act + EED require PUE and WUE reporting. USA: state by state, no federal framework. Asia: mixed. LATAM: virtually none — no obligation to report water consumption. The asymmetry is not accidental: it is the engine of today's deployment geography.
Blind spot
The global debate argues global averages about a phenomenon that is regional and distributional. A 100 MW datacenter is invisible in the world matrix and devastating in a 50,000-resident municipality. The right question is "which specific community pays the externality of the next model?".

The calculation lays bare two things. First: electricity is the least profitable line in the entire chain — and yet it is the one that triggers local conflict. Second: when a hyperscaler chooses between Virginia, Querétaro, and Quilicura, the operational difference on the electricity bill is on the order of cents per GPU-hour. What moves the decision is how fast a country signs the environmental permit.

A Chilean municipality of 50,000 with a 100 MW datacenter bears ~280 GWh/year of concentrated demand and ~6 billion liters of water if cooling is evaporative. The footprint spreads across the whole country in aggregate and onto 50,000 people in particular. LATAM, sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Southeast Asia are about to learn that the hard way.

If someone tells you AI is going to break the grid, ask them which grid. If someone tells you the consumption is trivial, ask them to tell that to the neighbor in Cerrillos, in Loudoun, and in Colón. The global figure and the local conflict live on different planets.

— P. C.

Datum of the week

22 years

From groundbreaking to first criticality

Time elapsed between the formal start of construction of India's Kalpakkam prototype fast breeder reactor (October 2004) and its first controlled fission chain reaction (April 6, 2026). The original schedule called for six years. The final cost overrun exceeds 3× the initial budget. India is now, alongside Russia and China, one of three countries with a commercial fast reactor in operation.
