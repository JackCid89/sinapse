§ 02

Cover

USA · Genetics · Biomedicine

The first in vivo CRISPR crosses Phase 3: what HAELO proves and what it leaves open

A single infusion, one silenced liver protein, 87 % fewer angioedema attacks. Intellia's lonvo-z isn't the first approved human gene-editing therapy, but it is the first to edit cells without removing them from the body — and to show meaningful clinical efficacy in a pivotal trial.

By the SINAPSE editorial teamMay 5, 2026

On April 27, Intellia Therapeutics announced results from HAELO, a Phase 3 trial enrolling 67 type I/II hereditary angioedema patients. The primary endpoint — monthly attack rate over 24 weeks — fell 87 % versus placebo. The tougher secondary endpoint — total absence of attacks and prophylaxis over the same period — was met by 62 % of lonvo-z recipients versus 11 % on placebo. Intellia opened a rolling BLA with the FDA and projects commercial launch in the first half of 2027.

What matters about HAELO isn't the disease. Hereditary angioedema affects between 1 in 50,000 and 1 in 100,000 people depending on country; effective prophylactics (lanadelumab, berotralstat) and acute treatments (icatibant, C1-inhibitor) already exist. What matters is the therapeutic architecture.

What lonvo-z actually does

The patient receives a single intravenous infusion. It contains lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) that physiologically concentrate in the liver via LDL-receptor uptake on hepatocytes. The LNPs encapsulate two RNAs: a messenger RNA encoding S. pyogenes Cas9 and a guide RNA targeting KLKB1, the prekallikrein gene. Once internalized by the hepatocyte, the cellular machinery translates the mRNA, the Cas9–sgRNA complex finds its target, cuts DNA, and non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) repair leaves an indel that inactivates the gene.

The interrupted cascade has been well understood since the 1990s: prekallikrein activates to kallikrein; kallikrein releases bradykinin from kininogen; bradykinin increases vascular permeability and triggers the attack. If prekallikrein isn't synthesized, the cascade doesn't start.

From ex vivo to in vivo

Casgevy, the first approved CRISPR therapy (FDA, December 2023), edits hematopoietic stem cells extracted from the patient, modifies them in the lab, and reinfuses them after myeloablative conditioning. The procedure takes months, requires specialized hospital infrastructure, costs near $1M per patient, and carries a toxicity profile inherited from autologous transplant.

Lonvo-z is given as an outpatient infusion. The operational difference is radical: a bag, a vein, a room. Adverse events reported at 28 weeks were predominantly infusion reactions, headache, and fatigue — all mild or moderate; no serious events were attributed to lonvo-z. The difference between "hospitalize the patient to rebuild their marrow" and "deliver an IV bag" is not an incremental improvement. It is a change of category.

What the trial doesn't prove

Three caveats matter. First, durability. NHEJ indels are permanent at the edited DNA level, but hepatocytes turn over: human hepatocyte half-life is on the order of 200 to 300 days. Whether plasma kallikrein stays suppressed at 5 or 10 years is unanswered.

Second, off-target editing. Intellia reports off-target profiles measured by CIRCLE-seq and GUIDE-seq with low signals, but genome-scale evaluation in living humans is methodologically hard.

Third, scaling to common diseases. The next targets — familial hypercholesterolemia (PCSK9), alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, hemochromatosis — widen the eligible universe by one to two orders of magnitude. That forces discussions HAELO didn't touch: price ($800,000/dose for 500,000 patients?), 20-year liability, and pharmacovigilance for treatments with irreversible DNA effects.

Geopolitics and regulation

The competitive picture has three protagonists: Intellia (Boston, MA), Verve Therapeutics (Boston, MA), and Beam Therapeutics (Cambridge, MA). The geographic concentration is striking. Outside Boston only China and, to a lesser extent, the UK and Switzerland show advanced-stage in vivo programs.

Three readouts will decide whether HAELO was inflection or anecdote. The first, September 2026: Verve VERVE-102 12-month follow-up data. The second, Q1 2027: the FDA decision on lonvo-z. The third, late 2027: the first Phase 2/3 in vivo trial in an indication with prevalence >1 in 1,000. If those three converge positively, last week was the start of the therapeutic paradigm that will distinguish the next decade of clinical genomics from the previous one.

§ 03

Papers

Four recent publications with summary, authorship, and why they matter.

MaterialsSuperconductivity

Direct imaging of fermion pairs in an ultracold atomic gas

MIT-Harvard team · Physical Review Letters · April 15, 2026

Quantum-gas microscopy visualization of the spatial correlation between opposite-spin fermions in a controlled analog of the attractive Hubbard model. Observed pairs show submicron synchronized motion that does not appear in the standard BCS solution and approaches BEC-like strong-coupling regimes.

BCS theory describes weak-coupling well but fails in the BCS-BEC crossover, relevant for understanding unconventional superconductors (cuprates, hydrides). Having a model system where you can see, particle by particle, how a pair emerges, gives a testbed for pairing theories beyond mean-field.

RoboticsEmbodied AI

ELLMER: sensorimotor robot control with an LLM and RAG over curated knowledge

University of Cambridge and collaborators · Nature Machine Intelligence · April 2026

Framework combining vision, force feedback, a reasoning large language model, retrieval-augmented generation over a curated robotic knowledge base, and a low-level controller. Demonstrates execution of unstructured kitchen tasks under unanticipated human perturbations.

Generalization in robotics fails when the environment deviates from training distribution. Pairing an LLM with a task knowledge base plus force control lets the robot reason about "what to do" without retraining the motor policy. One of the first peer-reviewed demonstrations that holds up outside the lab.

EcologyLatin AmericaClimate change

40 years of tree-diversity change in Amazonian and Andean forests

Fadrique et al. · University of Liverpool and RAINFOR network · Nature Ecology & Evolution · April 2026

Analysis of standardized inventories from more than 250 permanent plots across lowland and highland Amazonia and Andean forests over four decades. Results show an upward compositional reshuffle: species adapted to higher temperatures and seasonal drought gain ground over the historically dominant ones at each altitude.

The "Amazon tipping point" debate has been largely model-driven. These primary data indicate the transition is not a future event: it has been happening at low speed for decades. Direct implications for terrestrial-carbon models and for conservation criteria that assume compositional stability.

GeneticsLatin AmericaPopulation health

The genetic landscape of autism in Latin American populations

MEXPLO-ASD consortium · Nature Medicine · April 28, 2026

Analysis of ASD-associated variants in more than 15,000 individuals from Mexico, Peru, Colombia, Brazil, and US-based Latino diaspora. The cohort contributes rare variants not described in European databases and shows substantial overlap with European-ancestry ASD architecture, against the hypothesis of a sharply different etiology.

Clinical genomic reference bases are biased toward European ancestries; this work improves the equity of polygenic risk models for the continent. It also reduces the empirical base for hypotheses that assumed radically different genetic architectures for the same conditions across regions.

§ 06

Column

The open body: what changes when gene editing happens inside

By Jack CidMay 5, 2026

There is a change better understood with an image than with a metric. Until April 27, an approved human CRISPR therapy looked like a transplant: cell extraction, manipulation in a sterile dish, myeloablative conditioning, reinfusion. After April 27 another image is available: a transparent plastic bag connected to a peripheral vein, an outpatient room, a couple of hours, and an enzymatic system that rides the bloodstream to the organ it touches.

It is the difference between operating on an organ and dosing a drug. The FDA, payers, patients, ethical regulators, and insurers have spent the last five years learning to handle the first. The serious question is whether they are ready for the second. The honest answer is no.

The first focus that shifts is who receives gene therapy. As long as editing was ex vivo, the population ceiling was set by infrastructure: a hospital capable of myeloablative conditioning edits dozens of patients a year, not thousands. Intravenous administration breaks that ceiling. The targets being discussed — familial hypercholesterolemia, alpha-1 antitrypsin, hemochromatosis, monogenic metabolic syndrome — widen eligibility by one to two orders of magnitude. When a somatic-genome therapy can reach half a million patients, the regulatory conversation stops being about individuals and starts being about populations.

The second focus is temporal asymmetry. A classical drug is discontinued when a population-level adverse effect appears; the effect fades as patients stop taking it. An indel introduced by NHEJ in hepatocytes does not fade: it persists as long as the edited hepatocytes or their progeny persist. If at year 7 pharmacovigilance picks up a mutational signal at an unanticipated locus, the answer is not to pull the product. The answer is to live with the consequence.

The third focus is geopolitical. Lonvo-z, VERVE-102, Beam BEAM-302, and the rest of the advanced-stage in vivo programs come from the same four-kilometer radius around Kendall Square. A pricing decision in Boston carries anthropological echoes that a pricing decision on a tablet does not. Global governance of the somatic genome, in the strong sense of the word, still doesn't exist.

What we are celebrating this week in medical terms is real: a safe and effective therapy for a disabling disease in a single dose. What we are opening in social terms is wide enough not to lose sight of. The open body of the next decade will not be the surgical one. It will be chemical, controlled by enzymes traveling in the bloodstream reading DNA.

§ 07

Counterpoint

Gain of function: the most misnamed debate in contemporary biology

By Passiflora CaeruleaMay 5, 2026

«Gain-of-function research caused COVID» / «Gain-of-function research protects us from future pandemics». Two camps speaking past each other in US Congressional testimony, 2023–2026

Some debates start with the wrong name. "Gain of function" is, in molecular biology, an enormous label: any mutation that increases a function — from a slightly more efficient enzyme to a virus that learns to infect a new species. Political conversation, however, uses the term as a synonym for "research that might start a pandemic." That is a subset: what US regulation calls Enhanced Potential Pandemic Pathogens (ePPP). Conflating one with the other is not an academic detail.

Atlas of the problem · 7 dimensions
Countries in the headline
USA (NIH, NIAID), China (Wuhan Institute of Virology), Netherlands (Fouchier, H5N1 ferret studies, 2011), United Kingdom (Pirbright, Porton Down).
Countries in the actual story
India (BSL-4 under construction in Pune and Bhopal), Philippines (announced BSL-4), Brazil (BSL-3+ at Fiocruz), South Africa (NICD BSL-4), Kazakhstan, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, Australia (CSIRO). Of 18 BSL-4 planned by 2030, most are in Asia.
Who profits
NIH: ~$10-30M/year in ePPP-relevant grants. Pharma: academic GoF feeds antiviral and pre-pandemic vaccine pipelines. National governments: legitimize biodefense programs with adjacent military budgets. Insurers (Munich Re, Swiss Re): underwrite pandemic bonds priced on the program's own estimates.
Who pays
Communities near BSL-3+/4 labs with no notification framework. Health systems that carry the response when an incident occurs. Countries without their own BSL-4: depend on others' findings and transparency. Public trust in biomedicine: every uncommunicated incident erodes the political base of legitimate research.
5 / 10-year horizon
2028–2030: 18 new BSL-4 enter operation (most in Asia); global population with a national BSL-4 rises from ~50 % to ~70 %. Multilateral framework: WHO Pandemic Accord under negotiation. Accessible synthetic biology turns the question "who can do GoF?" from "which country?" to "which university lab?".
Regulatory gap
USA: 2024 USG Policy on DURC + ePPP. EU: nominally homogeneous ABSL framework. UK: HSE under ACDP. China: opaque internal framework; no external audit access. India / Brazil / South Africa: frameworks under construction. BWC: no independent verification mechanism since 1972. No global body maintains a comprehensive BSL-3+/4 registry.
Blind spot
The public debate treats GoF as a binary moral question when the operative question is one of distributed governance: how it gets audited, how risk is shared, how an incident is reported, what consequences follow non-reporting. The right question isn't whether the research should exist, but under what transparency regime it is defensible.

The picture leaves an uncomfortable conclusion for both sides: the question "should we do GoF?" is going out of style not because it has been answered, but because geography answered it for us. While the US debates moratoria, India builds two more BSL-4, China runs its own without external audit, and the Biological Weapons Convention still has no verification mechanism since 1972.

If someone tells you GoF caused the previous pandemic, ask what evidence would change the claim. If someone tells you GoF prevents the next one, ask when it last did. If someone tells you the problem is only Wuhan, ask where the next BSL-4 will open. The three questions return different silences. All three are useful.

— P. C.

Paper 01Cooper pairs in an ultracold atomic gas

DisciplineCondensed matter
RegionNorth America · MIT–Harvard
PublicationPhys. Rev. Lett. · April 15, 2026
Summary
Direct quantum-gas microscopy imaging of pair correlations in a fermionic atomic gas in the BCS-BEC crossover; pairs show submicron synchronized motion not described by the mean-field BCS solution.
Methodology
Fermionic atomic system (likely 6Li or 40K) in an optical lattice; Feshbach resonance to tune the attractive interaction; quantum-gas microscopy for single-particle resolution.
Limitations
Simplified attractive Hubbard model; no phonon dynamics; extrapolation to solid-state limited by absence of an ionic lattice.
