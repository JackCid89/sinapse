Cosmology

The Hubble tension graduates from anomaly to crisis

The H0DN collaboration published the most precise joint measurement so far of the Universe's local expansion rate: 73.50 ± 0.81 km/s/Mpc. The value inferred from the early Universe remains at ~67-68. In plain terms: the speed at which the Universe expands today doesn't match what models predict from the echo of the Big Bang. After a decade of improving measurements, the mismatch holds. Something is missing from the standard cosmological model.

Cover

Cosmology · International

The Hubble tension is no longer noise: the standard model has to change

The H0DN collaboration combined five independent ways of measuring distances in the local Universe and obtained 73.50 ± 0.81 km/s/Mpc. That value is 5.8 σ away from the standard cosmological model prediction. After a decade, the discrepancy consolidates as a model failure, not a thermometer failure.

By the SINAPSE editorial team · May 12, 2026

For years, the decent way to discuss the Hubble tension was as an anomaly. The Universe, measured directly with Cepheids, type Ia supernovae, and water masers, appears to expand at about 73 km/s/Mpc. The same constant inferred from the cosmic microwave background measured by Planck plus the ΛCDM model gives 67-68. The difference is on the order of 9%, and many times it has been explainable as a bias from some particular calibrator or as an artifact of combining different telescopes. The H0DN publication this week closes that door. When five independent measurement paths — Cepheids, tip of the red giant branch, J-band asymptotic giant branch, masers, supernovae — are linked and the full system is forced to be self-consistent, the result doesn't move: 73.50 ± 0.81 km/s/Mpc, at 5.8 σ from the value inferred from the early Universe.

Why the number matters

The Hubble constant, H0, sets the time scale of everything computed in cosmology: the age of the Universe, the amount of dark matter present, the rate at which galaxies separate. It isn't a free parameter to be conveniently tuned: the standard cosmological model, ΛCDM, predicts it from first principles once the matter density, spatial curvature, and dark-energy density are fixed. If the model prediction differs from the direct measurement, something is missing. That has been the problem for a decade, but it could have been local (a poorly measured calibrator) or systemic (an incomplete theory). The H0DN result tilts decisively toward the second.

Precision is the relevant piece. The error band of ±0.81 km/s/Mpc is the tightest ever obtained for a direct measurement. It is 2.3 times more precise than the HST + JWST combined value published by SH0ES in 2024 and halves the Pantheon+ confidence interval. That precision means the disagreement with Planck can no longer be explained by overlapping error bars: the two distributions are clearly disjoint.

What could be missing from the model

If ΛCDM is incomplete here, the theoretical candidates to mend it are three, each with its own price.

The first is early dark energy (EDE): an additional energy component active only a few thousand years before recombination, which recalibrates the sound horizon scale and, with it, the prediction of H0. It is the best numerical fit and the most debated proposal since 2019. It matches the cosmic microwave data but requires fine tuning that many theorists consider inelegant.

The second is the effective number of relativistic species (ΔNeff): incorporating additional neutrino species or non-standard relativistic particles in the early Universe. It partially resolves the tension but introduces conflicts with primordial nucleosynthesis.

The third, more radical, is a modification of gravity at cosmological scales. f(R) families, massive gravity, or interacting dark-matter sectors are on the table. The cost of admitting them is high: it implies that the success of General Relativity at solar-system, cluster, and gravitational-lensing scales does not carry over unchanged to the scale of the entire Universe.

"After a decade of improving thermometers, the difference doesn't move. Either nature is hiding a surprise on a time scale we are not measuring, or the standard model of cosmology has a missing piece."

What it still doesn't resolve

The H0DN result does not determine which of the three families — EDE, extra neutrinos, modified gravity — is correct. For that, another thing is needed: measuring sigma8 (the amplitude of matter perturbations at 8 Mpc) with comparable precision. Current data suggest there is also a second, smaller tension in sigma8: the value inferred from the cosmic microwave background is slightly larger than that observed in weak-lensing surveys like DES and KiDS. If the H0 solution involves EDE, sigma8 becomes more coherent. If it involves modified gravity, not necessarily. That convergence or non-convergence is the next test.

The second open issue is the role of baryonic matter at intermediate scales. The actual amount of gas trapped between clusters — the "intracluster medium" — is measured with uncertainty by X-ray and radio groups and enters the error bar of H0DN. A future recalibration of that amount could move the measurement by up to 0.5 km/s/Mpc, reducing the disagreement but not closing it.

How the story will unfold over the next 24 months

Three readouts will decide how the field reorganizes. First, second half of 2026: Euclid publishes its first complete cosmological sample, with weak lensing and galaxy clustering measuring sigma8 and the dark-matter fraction simultaneously. If Euclid confirms the sigma8 tension at the same significance, convergence toward EDE becomes nearly mandatory.

Second, 2027: LSST/Rubin Observatory enters scientific operation and begins producing type Ia supernovae at daily cadence, which halves the uncertainty in the supernova branch of the distance model.

Third, 2028: CMB-S4 takes microwave-background measurements with sensitivity to detect signatures of EDE or ΔNeff directly. That measurement acts as arbiter: if EDE exists, it leaves a footprint in microwave-background polarization that CMB-S4 can see.

For now, the summary is this: what for ten years looked like an instrument problem now has the face of a physics problem. The Universe is expanding faster than the model predicts, and the question isn't whether the measurement is wrong but what the theory is missing. Contemporary cosmology hasn't been this open since the discovery of dark energy in 1998.

§ 03

Papers

Four recent publications with summary, authorship, and why they matter.

CosmologyH0DN

Joint measurement of the local expansion rate of the Universe: 73.50 ± 0.81 km/s/Mpc

H0 Distance Network Collaboration · Nature Astronomy · May 2026

Synthesis of five independent distance ladders (Cepheids, TRGB, JAGB, water masers, type Ia supernovae) over 42 common host galaxies. The "distance network" eliminates individual calibration biases through cross-consistency and reduces 2.3× the uncertainty vs SH0ES 2024.

Why it matters: the final figure is 5.8 σ from the value inferred by Planck from the cosmic microwave background. It confirms the tension is real, not instrumental, and redirects the field toward active search for extensions of ΛCDM (early dark energy, additional neutrinos, modified gravity).

BiomedicineMetabolism

A molecular switch in brown fat activates adaptive thermogenesis

McGill University team · Nature Metabolism · May 2026

Identification of a regulatory complex (a brown-adipose-tissue-specific protein kinase) that controls the transition between energy storage and caloric dissipation. Functional demonstration in mouse model and partial validation in human biopsies after controlled cold exposure.

Why it matters: brown adipose tissue is one of the few human organs whose function can be pharmacologically modulated to increase energy expenditure without affecting appetite. It is a therapeutic target complementary to GLP-1 agonists (Ozempic, Wegovy), with a different mechanism and potentially fewer gastrointestinal side effects.

AstrobiologyBiosignatures

A universal chemical signature to distinguish life from non-life in unknown samples

Walker, Cleaves et al. · Nature Astronomy · May 2026

Statistical analysis of amino acid and fatty acid distributions in 134 biological and abiotic samples. Living systems exhibit a systematic "asymmetric complexity" (favoring some compounds and rejecting others) that does not appear in prebiotic chemistry. The metric detects life with 87% accuracy without requiring prior knowledge of local biochemistry.

Why it matters: missions to Mars (Perseverance, Tianwen-3), Enceladus, and Europa have collected or will collect samples where the key question is whether biotic chemistry is present. A metric agnostic to terrestrial biochemistry avoids the "look for what we know" bias and offers a quantifiable biosignature test.

VirologyVulnerabilities

The mechanism by which enteroviruses take control of cellular translation

UMBC team · Cell Host & Microbe · May 2026

Structural characterization of how enteroviruses (polio, EV-D68, enterovirus 71, rhinovirus) redirect human ribosomes to translate their own viral RNA. They identify a pharmacological blocking point common to the family, previously considered unreachable.

Why it matters: enteroviruses cause everything from the common cold to poliomyelitis, acute myocarditis, and encephalitis in children. No approved pan-enteroviral antiviral exists. The identified target is a candidate for a first generation of broad-spectrum antivirals against the group, conceptually comparable to anti-influenza therapy with oseltamivir.

§ 06

Column

The new Standard Oil of silicon

By Jack Cid

In 1911 the United States Supreme Court forced the dissolution of Standard Oil. By then the company controlled 90% of refining in the country, and half the world's crude passed through its pipes. The ruling rested on a phrase that contemporary regulatory language is still chasing: restraint of trade. What was visible in 1911 was not formal monopoly but structural dependence. Whoever needed kerosene, lubricant, or gasoline knew that sooner or later they would pay the price Standard chose to set, because there was no other path to the resource.

In 2026, one Taiwanese company manufactures roughly 92% of the world's advanced semiconductors. Its market cap has just crossed two trillion dollars. Its annual capex of $31 billion is comparable to Honduras's GDP. Its fabrication capacity for top customers is booked through end of 2027 — meaning if a company is not Apple, NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm, MediaTek, or Broadcom, it cannot buy advanced silicon from TSMC this decade, unless it pays a preferred-customer premium. That is the situation.

The analogy is not rhetorical. Three conditions sustain it. The first is geographic concentration: TSMC controls 92% of nodes ≤7 nm; Samsung Foundry and SMIC, the other two serious players, hold the rest. The second is chain captivity: making an advanced chip also requires CoWoS packaging, also dominated by TSMC; HBM, dominated by Samsung and SK Hynix; and EUV lithography, an absolute ASML monopoly. Each step of the process is a bottleneck. The third is political asymmetry: TSMC is not just a company, it is a strategic asset that the Taiwanese government protects, that the US government has decided to partially replicate, and that China has declared an objective for a decade without reaching it.

Standard Oil had incipient competitors (Gulf, Texaco) that the dissolution allowed to grow. TSMC has none. Intel announced four years ago it would enter as foundry of advanced processes and has suffered successive delays; Samsung, technically capable, loses top customers due to yield problems on sub-3 nm nodes; Rapidus, the Japanese project, hopes to deliver commercial 2 nm in 2027 and will have to prove competitive density and cost. None is positioned to capture the next five years. The market, in practice, is one and a half: TSMC dominant, Samsung as second-tier backup, the rest irrelevant.

The difference from 1911, however, is important. Standard Oil concentrated control of a natural resource. TSMC concentrates control of accumulated know-how. When you break up a resource monopoly, the pieces still hold value. When you break up — if it were possible — a monopoly of technical knowledge, the pieces take years to reach the productivity of the original. The TSMC capacity in Arizona, replicated at record speed with CHIPS subsidies, already has the original's yield. But internal reports speak of 12 to 18 months per node to match the learning economies. That difference sets the real limit of any "friend-shoring" strategy.

What this makes clear is that concentration cannot be resolved by regulatory will alone. It isn't 1911. A judicial decision in Washington does not produce more chips. The only path really capable of altering market geometry is technical: that on some of the next three nodes — A14, A10, A7 — Samsung or an unexpected actor match TSMC's yield with competitive economics. If that doesn't happen before 2030, concentration becomes structural for an entire generation of technology. If it does, the ecosystem breathes again.

Meanwhile, the problem is not just economic. A single war, a single earthquake, a single major interruption on a Pacific island of 36,000 square kilometers can derail the entire digital economy of the planet. No twentieth-century regulator would have tolerated that exposure. We accept it partly because we grew inside it, partly because the alternatives we know — disperse capacity, diversify technologically — demand timelines no elected government finds politically rewarding. But the fragility remains, constant, and one day it will hand in its invoice.

Counterpoint

Every week, Passiflora Caerulea unpacks a structural debate in science and technology with data, original calculations, and a multidimensional atlas.

Open weights is not open source: the real geometry of "open AI"

By Passiflora Caerulea

"China is winning the open AI race" / "Open source is democratizing artificial intelligence" Recurring phrasings in Reuters, FT, MIT Technology Review, April–May 2026

The vocabulary of the debate has already been captured, and that is half the problem. When a headline says "open source AI" it is probably talking about something that the Open Source Initiative, the body that has legally defined the term since 1998, would not consider open source. The OSAID 1.0 definition published in October 2024 requires three things: published weights, complete training code, and sufficiently detailed training data. None of the models that slip into public discourse — DeepSeek R1, Llama 4, Qwen 3.5, Mistral Large 3, Gemma 3 — publishes the data. What they publish are the weights. That, technically, is open weights, not open source. The difference matters.

Atlas of the problem

Countries in the headline
USA (Meta Llama, Mistral via Microsoft, "frontier closed" OpenAI/Anthropic), China (DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM, Kimi). The "US vs China" frame dominates coverage.
Countries in the actual story
Singapore (SEA-LION built on Qwen 3.5), Malaysia (MyAI on DeepSeek V4), Brazil (Maritalk Open on Qwen and Llama mixed), India (Bharat AI with OpenHathi on Llama), UAE (Falcon, in-house, based on Arabic data), Vietnam (PhoGPT on Llama), Kenya (Jacaranda Health uses open models via cheap API), Nigeria (LangBot Africa, via OpenAI with credits). Real adoption is where the headline doesn't look.
Who profits
Chip makers (NVIDIA ~75% margin H100/Blackwell; SMIC in China with state subsidies). Hyperscalers serve open models via API (AWS Bedrock, Azure AI, Google Vertex, all collect 60-70% operating margin on compute). Originating labs (Meta, DeepSeek, Alibaba) capture strategic positioning and attract talent. Local integrators (Capgemini India, Globant LATAM) charge for fine-tuning and deployment.
Who pays
Countries adopting open weights without data audit: inherit biases from majority Anglo and Chinese cohorts. Languages with under 100M speakers (Quechua, Swahili, Bengali dialects): barely appear in released weights; "sovereign" models based on open weights inherit that silence. Communities with sensitive data (medical, judicial) in countries without protection frameworks: use models whose security implications no one has audited.
5 / 10-year horizon
Technical convergence between open and closed has already happened and deepens (five open frontier families). What follows is regulatory divergence: the EU will begin requiring dataset traceability under the AI Act; the US will remain in a patchwork of executive orders; China will keep centralized model registration. LATAM and Africa will enter the cycle when external audit requirements arrive, likely via WHO-WTO pacts for medical/financial AI.
Regulatory gap
EU: AI Act + GPAI requires dataset reporting (at least summaries). US: voluntary, fragmented, changes by administration. China: models must register with CAC before public deployment; open weights not exempt. India / Brazil / Mexico: frameworks in draft, no data transparency obligation. Sub-Saharan Africa: except South Africa, no specific framework; personal-data protection via general law. The Global South adopts before it regulates.
Blind spot
The debate treats "open" as synonymous with "sovereign". It isn't. A country that trains a sovereign model on Chinese weights inherits invisible moderation decisions, linguistic biases, and security vulnerabilities introduced upstream. Real technical sovereignty requires open data + open code + external audit, not just receiving the weights. Fewer models meet that than the headlines count.
What the headlines say
"Five Chinese open models reach frontier in May 2026"
What the technical definitions show
Of 12 models called "open" in this week's press, 0 meet the OSAID 1.0 definition (weights + code + data). All publish weights. Most publish some of the code. None publish the training datasets.
0 / 12

Let's do the concrete math, because numbers help see the asymmetry. Approximate cost of serving an open Llama-3 70B or DeepSeek V3 model per day to a user making 50 queries: on AWS Bedrock, ~$0.12 at list price. On self-hosted infrastructure with a rented H100 ($2.5/hour) at 30% average utilization, the cost drops to ~$0.02 per user-day. Cost of fine-tuning the same model for a domain (say, Paraguayan jurisprudence): ~$300-1,200 in cloud compute, one week of time, a team of 2 engineers. That is: technically, the entry barrier is trivial for a reasonable government agency or university.

The barrier isn't technical, it is data. For that model to be useful in Paraguay, fine-tuning on local jurisprudence isn't enough; one has to evaluate which biases it inherited from pre-training. How much Spanish-language jurisprudence did it see? How much continental European vs Anglo-Saxon legal tradition? How many Guaraní documents passed through its tokens? The answer for all current open models is: we don't know, because the data isn't published. That is why "open weights" is not sovereignty: the inheritance is blind.

The picture leaves a conclusion that doesn't show up in headlines: the vast majority of the "open" that reaches the Global South today is open weights, not open source. The difference is the difference between buying a recipe without knowing what its ingredients are and being able to inspect the cultivation of each one. For many everyday uses, that doesn't matter. For real technical sovereignty — medical, judicial, electoral, defense data — it does, very much.

What the public debate gets wrong is presenting the dilemma as "open vs closed" or as "China vs US". The real dilemma is between visible dependence (single-vendor closed model: I know I depend, I negotiate the price) and invisible dependence (open model with secret data: I think I don't depend, but I have inherited the bias of whoever gave me the weights). The second is harder to manage because it doesn't feel like dependence.

If they tell you your country "has its own AI because it runs on Qwen", ask how much local jurisprudence Qwen processed during pre-training. If they tell you "open democratizes", ask which data was audited before adopting it. If they tell you "it is sovereign because you now have the weights", ask where the moderation decisions that came with those weights live. The three questions return useful silences.

— P. C.

Paper 01 · H0DN — Local Hubble constant
Summary
Combination of five distance ladders (Cepheids, TRGB, JAGB, masers, type Ia supernovae) over 42 common host galaxies. Result: H0 = 73.50 ± 0.81 km/s/Mpc, 5.8 σ from the value inferred by Planck.
Methodology
Self-consistent "distance network": each measurement path is calibrated against the others as simultaneous constraints, eliminating individual biases. JWST + HST + ground-based data.
Limitations
Still limited to the local Universe (z < 0.1); requires statistical isotropy assumption; future recalibration of intracluster gas may move ±0.3 km/s/Mpc.
