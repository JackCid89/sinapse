§ 02

Cover

Africa · Global health · Immunisation

The RTS,S malaria vaccine works in the real world: now someone has to pay for it

Ten years after the first Phase III trial, the malaria vaccine reduces child mortality under real deployment conditions. The obstacle is no longer science.

By the SINAPSE editorial teamMay 19, 2026

The study published in The Lancet on May 8 closes a cycle that began in 2015. The first three countries to roll out routine deployment of the RTS,S/AS01 vaccine against Plasmodium falciparum — Ghana, Kenya and Malawi — have now accumulated enough vaccinated cohorts and geographic controls to compare child mortality with statistical power. The signal is clear: children who receive the four doses die less often, and most of the benefit appears where transmission is high and seasonal.

What matters is not only the magnitude of the effect but the setting in which it is measured. This is not a controlled trial with extra staff and bespoke logistics: it is routine deployment in real clinics, with imperfect cold-chains, variable attendance, and a vaccine that has to find space in an already packed childhood schedule. RTS,S is the first vaccine against a parasitic disease whose effectiveness has been confirmed outside the laboratory.

The bottleneck is no longer evidence

Twenty-four African countries have incorporated RTS,S or the newer R21/Matrix-M into their immunisation calendars. Ten million children are now the annual target. Yet most of the programmes are running below their planned national scale-up, and the reason is the same almost everywhere: insufficient funding. Gavi has asked donors for USD 8.5 billion for the 2026-2030 cycle; pledges committed by May fall short, and the deficit shows up first in vaccine resupply and, above all, in community-health staffing.

R21, manufactured by the Serum Institute of India at a substantially lower price, has relieved part of the supply pressure that early RTS,S pilots could not have foreseen. But the more structural problem remains: a disease that kills almost half a million African children a year depends, for prevention, on a financing mechanism beating to the rhythm of European elections and Washington priority shifts.

What the next paper will look at

The next endpoint — evidence being prepared for 2027 — is the aggregate effect on transmission in high-coverage zones, where the “herd” effect on unvaccinated under-5s should appear if vaccination at scale interrupts the parasite cycle. If it does, it would change the cost-effectiveness of programmes and the 5–10 year horizon of malaria as a chronic problem.

Until then, the editorial question is different. The data from May 8 close the doubt many donors had held about RTS,S since the Phase III trial. Turning that certainty into budget is, from here on, a political matter.

Papers

Three annotated reads.

ClimateGeodesyCoasts

Subsidence more than doubles present-day sea-level rise on densely populated coasts

High-resolution VLM observations · Nature · May 2026

Combining InSAR and GPS time-series, the paper decomposes relative sea-level rise into a eustatic component and local subsidence. In fourteen coastal megacities — from Jakarta to Alexandria — the sum decisively exceeds pure climate projections.

National adaptation plans calibrated solely against IPCC scenarios underestimate the real pace on coasts with groundwater extraction or recent reclamation.

ClimateAfricaHydrology

Extreme rainfall will intensify across Africa under all warming scenarios

Multi-model CMIP6 + observations · Nature · May 2026

The largest intensifications appear in the equatorial belt. The dominant driver is increased atmospheric moisture from radiative warming: more water available, shorter sinks, more violent events.

African urban infrastructure was designed against 20th-century rain cycles. That climate no longer exists across half the continent.

BiomedicineLiverMicrobiota

Phocaeicola dorei reduces liver fibrosis by regulating neutrophil clearance

Murine models and human samples · Nature · May 2026

A commensal gut bacterium orchestrates immune clearance of circulating neutrophils. In mice, its presence is associated with reduced hepatic fibrogenesis; in humans, its abundance correlates with clinical markers.

Opens a plausible probiotic route for metabolic liver disease, which currently has no approved treatment beyond lifestyle change.

§ 06

Column

The quantum apocalypse is not a Bitcoin problem: it's a problem with everything you send over the internet

By Jack CidMay 19, 2026

Every time a lab announces a quantum hardware advance — this week, IBM with Nighthawk; before that, Google with Willow — generalist press returns to the same headline: “the quantum apocalypse threatens Bitcoin.” It is a simplification that suits its repeaters because it has a clear villain and a familiar victim. The problem is that it hides the important question: which parts of the internet, exactly, stop working the day someone has a useful quantum machine?

The answer, ordered by pain, is this. What breaks first is asymmetric public-key cryptography — RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and elliptic curves (ECDSA, Ed25519, X25519). That is exactly what underpins almost everything we call “internet security.” Shor's algorithm, run on enough stable logical qubits, factors RSA and solves the discrete-log problem on elliptic curves in polynomial time. Bitcoin is an obvious victim, but it is far from the first.

Services that break on day D

Let's be concrete. A cryptographically relevant quantum machine invalidates the following in cascade:

HTTPS / TLS 1.3 — every secure browser connection depends on an elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman exchange to agree on the session key; whoever records the traffic today and runs Shor tomorrow reads it as plaintext. SSH — server access keys (Ed25519, ECDSA, RSA) break; every remotely administered machine becomes accessible to whoever held the public fingerprint. Corporate VPNs (IPsec, WireGuard, OpenVPN) use the same primitives in their handshakes; the tunnel becomes transparent. DNSSEC — the DNS digital signature is no longer trustworthy; resolution poisoning at a global scale. Code signing — iOS, Android, Windows and Linux updates, and package managers (apt, dnf, brew, npm, PyPI), sign binaries with RSA or ECDSA; forging a signature is equivalent to distributing malware with an official seal. X.509 certificates — the entire PKI, the chain of trust validating that nytimes.com is nytimes.com, collapses. S/MIME, PGP, Signal — encrypted email and end-to-end messaging expose their entire historical archive, because the attacker kept the ciphertext, waited for the machine, then decrypted; this is called harvest now, decrypt later, and it is already happening: there are sniffers preserving traffic for at least the last five years. WebAuthn, FIDO2, passkeys — modern passwordless authentication uses ECDSA signatures; current YubiKeys are vulnerable. EMV cards, 5G authentication, electronic passports — all signed with elliptic curves.

Symmetric cryptography (AES, ChaCha20) and hashes (SHA-256, SHA-3) suffer less. Grover's algorithm only delivers a quadratic speedup: AES-256 drops to AES-128 equivalent strength, which remains unreachable. Double the key length and the problem is solved. That means Bitcoin does not break entirely: the proof-of-work algorithm (SHA-256) survives. What falls is the signatures.

Enter the “quantum halving”

Here is the news few outside the crypto niche have registered: BIP-361, from Jameson Lopp and others, proposed in April 2026, contemplates deprecating all Bitcoin addresses with vulnerable signatures — ECDSA and Schnorr — within five years of the activation of the post-quantum output (BIP-360). Coins that do not migrate are frozen. Serious estimates: roughly 1.7 million BTC would be locked in, including approximately one million attributed to Satoshi Nakamoto, simply because pre-BIP-39 era keys cannot migrate to a new scheme.

Removing 1.7 million bitcoins from the circulating supply — out of 19.7 million mined — is an 8.6% cut. In the circles where this is discussed seriously, the manoeuvre has already been baptised the “quantum halving”: it is not a new emission like the four scheduled halvings, but its effect on accessible supply is comparable. It is not a loose analogy; it is exactly that. And unlike the scheduled halving, it is not voted on every four years: it is voted once, irreversibly.

Back to the starting point. That coverage concentrates on Bitcoin is a comfortable editorial choice. Bitcoin has visible prices, noisy personalities and a certain moral-fable flavour. What does not make headlines is what actually matters: every bank still issuing RSA-2048 certificates, every tax authority signing PDFs with ECDSA-P256, every hospital encrypting records with TLS 1.3 without a post-quantum layer is, right now, manufacturing cryptographic debt it will have to pay. NIST already standardised the replacements — ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, FN-DSA — in August 2024. Migration is an operational problem, not a research one.

The quantum apocalypse is, above all, an inflexible expiry date on classical cryptography. The question is not whether it will happen, but when, and to that one nobody dares answer. Meanwhile, Bitcoin will have its quantum halving — it will hurt a few billion dollars and Satoshi will dominate the conversation for a month. When HTTPS also breaks, no one will talk: things will simply stop working. We would do well to look there now.

Counterpoint

The second extraction: how the energy transition is rewriting the colonial geography of minerals

By Passiflora CaeruleaMay 19, 2026

“China dominates the lithium chain” / “Congo cobalt powers your iPhone” / “Indonesia plants its flag with nickel.” Variants interwoven through Reuters, FT, Bloomberg, April–May 2026

The market headline of the week is CATL: 46.6% Chinese market share, almost 30 GWh installed in April. Behind that gigawatt-hour there are three things: a chemistry, a process, and a geography. The first two are children of recent R&D investment. The third is old and hard to move: lithium comes out of Chile, Argentina and Australia; cobalt, from the Democratic Republic of the Congo; battery-grade nickel, from Indonesia and the Philippines. Conversion to precursors and cathodes happens, overwhelmingly, in China. BYD has built what it calls vertical integration “from the mine to the driver's seat”: stakes in Chilean and Argentinian lithium, long contracts with African producers, cathode plants in Asia, final assembly in Brazil.

Atlas of the problem · 7 dimensions
Countries in the headline
China, USA, EU: the “race” for the energy transition. Indonesia shows up occasionally as “the country that pushes back.”
Countries in the actual story
Chile, Argentina, Bolivia (lithium triangle), DRC (cobalt, 70% global), Indonesia, Philippines (battery-grade nickel), Australia, South Africa, Brazil (mixed minerals), Morocco (phosphate for LFP), Madagascar (graphite). The story has twenty protagonists; the headline names four.
Who profits
Intermediate processors (90% in China — Tianqi, Ganfeng, Huayou) and cell manufacturers (CATL, BYD, LG Energy, Panasonic, Samsung SDI; 15-25% gross margin). Car brands sit at the last link with the thinnest margin. Mining producers capture spot price, not industrial margin.
Who pays
Indigenous and local communities in mining zones: water (Andean salt flats), health (copper and cobalt dust), deferred restitution. End consumers in subsidised markets via taxes. The value of industrial learning does not stay in the producing country.
5 / 10-year horizon
5 years: raw-ore export bans spread from Indonesia to Chile, Brazil and DRC. 10 years: cathode and cell capacity outside China exceeds 20% global only if Western industrial policy sustains investment. Recycling starts to mature as an urban business around 2032-2035.
Regulatory gap
Inconsistent mineral traceability. Voluntary ESG standards. The EU Battery Regulation requires a carbon footprint and recycling but not margin capture. No multilateral framework treaty. The WTO debates export tariffs; the conversation moves at the usual pace.
Blind spot
Recycling. If the circular economy matures as projected, part of the extractive problem neutralises itself — but the margin then moves again: from mineral to urban refining know-how, which lives in European, Japanese and Chinese pilot plants. The sovereignty question shifts; it does not disappear.

Jakarta has spent three years demanding — via raw-ore export bans — that the transformation to battery-grade nickel happen on Indonesian soil. It has captured the first stage: there are now Chinese industrial parks in Sulawesi. But the next stage — cell, pack, integration into vehicle — still escapes. Chile is preparing a version of the same play with lithium, this time negotiating with several actors rather than handing one piece to the first bidder.

The underlying question is not whether the energy transition is good (it is) or whether decarbonisation requires minerals (it does). The question is whether the countries that own the resource will play the same historical role they played with oil and rubber — rentiers dependent on a single buyer — or whether they will force a different industrial geography. The answer will depend less on climate summits than on the export laws of the next five years.

Appendix · technical fact sheets

Details of the three papers in section 03.

Paper 01Subsidence and sea-level rise on populated coasts

DisciplineGeodesy, coastal climatology
RegionTropical & subtropical coastal megacities
PublicationNature · May 2026
Abstract
Combines high-resolution InSAR with continuous GPS series to decompose relative sea-level rise into eustatic and local subsidence components. In the fourteen cities studied, the sum exceeds pure IPCC projection by factors of up to 2.3×.
Methods
Fusion of radar constellations (Sentinel-1, ICEYE) with continuous GPS network. Per-pixel Bayesian modelling. Observation window 2015-2024.
Limitations
Subsidence is locally heterogeneous (up to two orders of magnitude in a few kilometres). Does not capture subsidence from very recent hydraulic megaprojects. Documented-city bias toward coastal Asia and Africa.

Paper 02Extreme rainfall intensification across Africa

DisciplineHydroclimatology
RegionEquatorial Africa, Sahel, East Africa
PublicationNature · May 2026
Abstract
Multi-model CMIP6 + gauge observations + ERA5 reanalysis. Intensification of extreme precipitation events is robust in SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios; maximum along the equatorial belt.
Methods
Analysis of > 99th-percentile R10mm events. Partial attribution to atmospheric humidity (Clausius-Clapeyron) vs. dynamics.
Limitations
Model spatial resolution limits interpretation at small-basin scale. Insufficient surface data across the Sahel and Central Africa.
