EcologyLatin AmericaFisheries policy

Chile opens the door to hunting sea lions

At the 21 July session of the Fisheries Committee, Chile's Undersecretariat of Fisheries signalled openness to lifting the moratorium on the South American sea lion, citing damage reported by southern artisanal fishers. On 30 July, Deputy Guillermo Valdés introduced a bill creating a National Plan for Population Control and Adaptive Management that would replace the total extraction ban with a management regime. The moratorium currently in force was renewed for thirty years and took effect on 11 November 2025.

A veda — a moratorium — is a temporary ban on extracting a species. Chile's covers marine mammals and draws no line between commercial hunting and damage control: killing a sea lion legally requires changing the law, not requesting a permit.
02P-02
AstrochemistryEuropeOrigins of life

The first sugar detected in the interstellar medium

A spectral survey of the molecular cloud G+0.693−0.027, near the Galactic Centre, identified erythrulose — a four-carbon sugar — using the Yebes 40 m and IRAM 30 m radio telescopes. It is the first direct observation of a sugar outside the Solar System. Models suggest it forms on dust grains by combining glycolaldehyde and ethylene glycol, two molecules already known in that environment.

Sugar existing does not imply life existing. It means part of the chemistry that precedes biology assembles in cold space, before there are stars or planets, and arrives at worlds already made.
03P-03
AstrophysicsBlack holes

Omega Centauri yields the first of its missing black holes

Drawing on twenty-three years of Hubble archival data plus recent Webb observations, a team identified oMEGACat BH-2: a 4.46-solar-mass black hole orbited by a star of 0.78 solar masses, the longest-period black hole binary known. Models say the cluster should host some ten thousand such objects, and until now not one had been confirmed.

The finding did not come from a new observation but from comparing two decades of images to measure a star's wobble. Archival astronomy is slow and cheap, and occasionally it wins.
04P-04
PalaeontologyAfricaEvolution

An Egyptian fossil ape shifts the map of great ape origins

A fossil ape described from material in northern Egypt reshuffles hypotheses about where the ancestors of today's great apes first appeared — a debate that has swung between Africa and Eurasia for decades. The North African record for that interval is sparse, and each specimen carries more weight than it would in a rich deposit.

In palaeontology, the absence of fossils in a region usually says more about where people have dug than about where the animals lived. Sampling bias is the discipline's permanent constraint.
05P-05
OncologyMexicoImmunology

UNAM tests a variable-epitope therapeutic vaccine in the lab

A team at Mexico's UNAM published results in Molecular Immunology on therapeutic vaccines built from variable epitope libraries: instead of aiming at a fixed target, they present the immune system with many slightly different versions of the same epitope, in order to track a mutating tumour. In laboratory models they eliminated breast cancer and inhibited metastasis. Human trials have not begun.

The distance between curing mice and curing people is historically enormous: the large majority of oncology therapies that work in murine models do not survive clinical phases. This is a real result and it is also the beginning of the road, not the end.
06P-06
AIAgricultureData

A model for the fields no commercial satellite looks at

Researchers in Cambridge's Department of Computer Science and Technology introduced Tessera, a machine learning tool aimed at closing the data gap for small and vulnerable farms in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, where commercial agronomic time series simply do not exist. The problem it attacks is not modelling but coverage: most of the world's hunger happens in the pixels nobody bought.

§ 02

Cover

Chile · U.S. · Astronomy · Exoplanets

The first habitable-zone rocky planet with a confirmed atmosphere: and it was measured from Atacama

A Harvard-led team detected helium escaping the atmosphere of LHS 1140 b using the Magellan telescopes in Chile. It is the first confirmation of an atmosphere on a rocky planet inside its star's habitable zone. It is not a detection of life, of water, or of breathable air.

By the SINAPSE editorial deskAugust 4, 2026

On 16 July, Science published the detection of helium escaping the upper atmosphere of LHS 1140 b, a super-Earth orbiting a red dwarf 48 light-years away in the constellation Cetus. The work, led by Collin Cherubim — who has just completed his doctorate in planetary sciences at Harvard — rests on a transit observed in September 2024 with the 6.5-metre Magellan telescopes at Las Campanas Observatory in northern Chile, fitted with a recently installed high-resolution spectrograph. It is the first confirmed atmosphere on a rocky planet located in its star's habitable zone.

It is worth understanding why helium matters. When a planet passes in front of its star, a tiny fraction of the starlight filters through its atmosphere before reaching us, and each gas leaves a signature at a specific colour. Escaping helium produces a characteristic signature and, above all, produces a signature that only makes sense if there is an atmosphere to escape from. The logic is indirect but robust: we do not see the air, we see the air leaving.

What the result does not say

First, «habitable zone» is an orbital definition, not a biological one: it describes the distance at which liquid water would be thermodynamically possible on the surface, and nothing more. Second, no water was detected, no oxygen, no molecule associated with biological processes; helium was detected, the second simplest element in the universe. Third, the star is a red dwarf, and red dwarfs are violent: they emit flares that erode atmospheres over billions of years. That this planet retains anything after more than three billion years is precisely what makes it interesting — and also what raises the suspicion that the atmosphere may be a heavy remnant rather than a habitable envelope.

Why it matters beyond astronomy

The operational detail is as relevant as the result: this was done from the ground, with high-resolution transit spectroscopy, without consuming any Webb time — the most contested resource in contemporary astronomy. For four years the community assumed that characterising rocky planet atmospheres was the space telescope's exclusive territory. This work shows that a new spectrograph mounted on a thirty-year-old telescope can reach where only a ten-billion-dollar machine was thought to reach. Webb now has a concrete target: the next four to five years of data will look for water, and if it appears, the atmosphere goes from existing to being interesting.

NeuroscienceNorth America

A third lesion turns up in Alzheimer's brains

Mitochondrial accumulation and lysosomal dysfunction result in mitochondrial plaques in Alzheimer's disease · Nature Neuroscience · 29 July 2026

A University of Minnesota team describes «mitochondrial plaques»: accumulations of damaged mitochondria inside swollen neuronal extensions. They form because cellular recycling starts and does not finish — the lysosomes that should digest the damaged mitochondria are themselves impaired, and the material piles up. They appear in two independent mouse models and in post-mortem human brain tissue.

If these plaques arise before beta-amyloid ones, as the authors suggest, the disease timeline would have a chapter earlier than the one currently targeted pharmacologically — which would help explain why anti-amyloid antibodies deliver so little in the clinic.

PhysicsPhotonicsEurope

A photonic time crystal, and light becomes modulable on trillionth-second scales

Plasmonic Metamaterial Time Crystal · Nature · 29 July 2026

A consortium from École Polytechnique, Collège de France and Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf built the first all-optical photonic time crystal: micrometre-scale gold structures on an indium antimonide semiconductor whose reflectivity is periodically modulated on ultrafast timescales. The setup is driven by TELBE, HZDR's superradiant terahertz source, and cuts photon losses in the terahertz range by more than half.

An ordinary crystal orders matter in space; this one orders optical properties in time. It opens the door to modulators, terahertz lasers and ultrafast optical computing — with the usual caveat: it is a laboratory experiment relying on a terahertz source that exists in very few places on Earth.

ChemistryNorth AmericaAsia

Hydrogen straight from mixed plastic, with no sorting first

Selective and direct hydrogen generation from mixed plastic waste via alkaline thermal treatment with inherent carbon storage · PNAS · 6 July 2026

Work co-led by UCLA Samueli and Ewha Womans University (South Korea) converts a mixture of three mass-use plastics into high-purity hydrogen through alkaline thermal treatment, at temperatures far below those of conventional gasification. The carbon does not leave as CO₂: it stays trapped in solid mineral form.

Chemical recycling's bottleneck was never the chemistry but the sorting: separating mixed plastics is expensive and often uneconomic. A process that tolerates the mixture attacks the step that defeats the others. What is missing, as always, is whether it scales and at what energy cost.

AI · Scientific method

Two multi-agent systems that propose hypotheses and test them

The 9 July issue of Nature carried two independent systems: Co-Scientist, from Google DeepMind, built on Gemini, which generated therapeutic candidates for acute myeloid leukaemia; and Robin, from FutureHouse, which combines literature search agents with data analysis agents and reached candidates for dry age-related macular degeneration. Both close the loop: hypothesis, proposed experiment, reading of results, revised hypothesis.

Quantum computing

A silicon processor that corrects its own errors without help from above

HRL Laboratories published in Nature a silicon quantum processor with an integrated cryogenic control chip that performs error correction autonomously, with no room-temperature control electronics in the loop. Control errors drop by an order of magnitude relative to previous demonstrations with this qubit type, and error suppression improves roughly fivefold as qubits are added to the repetition code.

AI · Agriculture

Tessera, or the problem of the data nobody collected

The model Cambridge presented on 7 July targets smallholdings in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia. The constraint is not architectural: commercial agronomic series cover the agriculture that already has capital, and most food insecurity occurs outside that coverage.

§ 06

Column

The hypothesis nobody had: science without an author, accountability without an owner

By Jack CidAugust 4, 2026

The two multi-agent systems Nature published on 9 July do something that had not been demonstrated in the same closed loop until now: they generate a hypothesis, propose the experiment that would test it, read the result and rewrite the hypothesis. Co-Scientist reached candidates for acute myeloid leukaemia that way; Robin, candidates for dry macular degeneration. The results are verifiable, they are published, and they do not depend on anyone believing anything.

My worry is not that the machine gets it wrong. It is that it gets it right, and that there is nobody behind the getting-it-right.

Science does not run on truth; it runs on attributable accountability

The scientific publishing system is, at bottom, a system of imputation. A name signs, a name answers. When a paper falls — and papers fall often — the retraction has an addressee: somebody has to explain the data, defend the method, accept the error, carry the reputational cost. That cost is what makes the method work, far more than any epistemological norm. Peer review does not detect fraud; it establishes that somebody will have to answer if there is any.

A hypothesis generated by a multi-agent system does not break that arrangement outright, because today there are researchers who sign the work. But it erodes it on the quiet side: the signatory stops being the author of the idea and becomes the operator of the process that produced it. Their responsibility, without changing name, changes nature — from «I answer for what I thought» to «I answer for what I validated». And validating is cheaper than thinking, especially when the volume of hypotheses to validate grows faster than the time of whoever validates.

The issue is not speed

This issue's cover is the useful counterexample. A doctoral student spent years preparing the measurement of a helium escape on a planet 48 light-years away, with a spectrograph mounted on a thirty-year-old telescope, in order to state something minimal and solid: that planet has an atmosphere. There is no agent that optimises such patience, because patience is not a bottleneck worth removing: it is the mechanism by which somebody comes to understand what they are measuring. The rate of hypothesis generation was never science's limiting factor. The limiting factor is understanding, and understanding does not parallelise.

I am not proposing we slow anything down, and it would be absurd to do so with two papers on the table showing real results. I am proposing that the institutional question be posed now, while it is cheap: when a hypothesis generated by a system and validated by a laboratory turns out to be false and has cost a clinical trial, who answers? If the answer is «the laboratory», then we need the laboratory to have understood the hypothesis, not merely to have executed it. And if the answer is «nobody», we will have built machinery that produces knowledge at an admirable rate and cannot account for any of its parts. This week's Counterpoint is about exactly the same thing by another route: deciding fast about systems that were never understood.

§ 07

Counterpoint

Mao's sparrows are swimming off Chiloé

Chile is weighing the lifting of a thirty-year moratorium in order to «control the population» of sea lions. The first national census says that population is stable. The experiment has already been run, at least five times, and it went wrong every time.

By Passiflora CaeruleaAugust 4, 2026

Let us start with the name, because the name is already the argument. The bill Deputy Guillermo Valdés introduced on 30 July creates a «National Plan for Population Control and Adaptive Management» of the South American sea lion, and amends the General Fisheries Law to replace the total extraction ban with a management regime. Nine days earlier, in the Fisheries Committee, the Undersecretariat had signalled openness to lifting the moratorium. And the moratorium in question — thirty years, in force since 11 November 2025 — is eight and a half months old.

«Population control» presupposes a population out of control. To the data, then. The first national sea lion census, carried out by the University of Valparaíso using aerial surveys and drones in the summer of 2019, estimated some 123,000 South American sea lions across 216 haul-out sites, 64 of them breeding colonies. In the northern zone abundance has oscillated for twenty-three years between 39,000 and 49,000 animals; in the southern zone, from Los Ríos to Aysén, between 50,000 and 67,000. The technical description of that series is not «explosion»: it is «stable situation with moderate oscillations». The bill carries in its title a diagnosis that the Chilean state's own census does not support (Subpesca).

The precedent nobody wants to cite

In 1958, China launched the Four Pests Campaign. Rats, flies, mosquitoes and sparrows. The sparrows made the list for a perfectly reasonable and perfectly incomplete reason: they eat grain. Millions of people went out banging pots and drums so the birds could not land and would fall from exhaustion; nests were torn down and eggs broken. Around one billion sparrows died. What did not enter the calculation is that the adult sparrow eats grain but feeds its chicks insects. Without sparrows, insect outbreaks surged across the very crops the campaign meant to protect. The most recent econometric work attributes to the eradication a fall of between 8 % and 9 % in agricultural yields, roughly 20 % of the total agricultural decline of the Great Famine and more than two million deaths — some 6 % of the total (University of Chicago). In 1960 the government removed the sparrow from the list and replaced it with bedbugs. The correction arrived two years and two million people late.

One might object that Mao is a rhetorical scarecrow: a dictatorship without science, a mass campaign, another scale entirely. Granted. Which is why it is worth reviewing the other four cases, all of them with ministries, biologists and budgets.

Four more runs of the same experiment

Yellowstone, 1926. The last wolf pack was exterminated to protect game. Elk multiplied, stripped willows and aspens, beavers lost their material and collapsed, and coyotes — with no competition — overran the park. The 1995 reintroduction reversed the cascade: where the wolves returned, coyotes fell by close to 80 %. Sixty-nine years to undo a decision taken in a single season.

Namibia and South Africa. Namibia culls on the order of 86,000 seals a year to protect its fishery. South Africa banned the cull in 1990 and its fishery recorded no economic impact. It is a thirty-six-year natural experiment, control group included, and the result points to overfishing rather than the seal as the underlying problem. It almost never appears in the debate.

Columbia River, United States. The closest case to Chile's: sea lions that learned to eat threatened salmon at the bottlenecks created by dams. Under Section 120 of the Marine Mammal Protection Act, three states and six tribes are authorised to remove up to 540 California sea lions and 176 Steller sea lions (NOAA Fisheries). The stated cost: about 38,000 dollars per sea lion removed and about 203 dollars per salmon recovered. And the salmon remains threatened, because what blocks the river is not the sea lions: it is the dams.

Australia. The dingo fence, 5,614 kilometres, is the largest accidental ecological experiment on the planet, with two comparable sides. On the dingo-free side there is lower diversity of small marsupials and reptiles, kangaroo populations running unchecked, and conditions that favour the very rabbit the fence was meant to stop. Removing the predator did not leave the ecosystem the same minus one animal: it left it simpler and more fragile.

What Chilean science says, and it is published

Maritza Sepúlveda, of the Institute of Biology at the University of Valparaíso, warns of the same thing the four previous cases show, in the language of trophic ecology: removing the predator lets dominant competitors displace the rest, and the result is poorer food webs with less diversity. She adds the operational point, which ought to close the discussion: international experience does not support the claim that killing pinnipeds reduces conflict with artisanal fishing (Inforegión).

And here it is worth not caricaturing the fisher. The damage is real: torn nets, lost catches, income that disappears overnight, and a state that has spent years offering nothing better than patience. A fisher from Los Lagos asking for the moratorium to be lifted is not being irrational; they are the one actor in the system paying the full cost of the problem. The irrationality is not in the demand: it is in the answer being offered.

Because southern hake is not scarce because of sea lions. It is scarce after decades of extraction above replacement, and nobody with data in hand disputes that. Killing the predator is the cheapest policy to announce — visible, immediate, with an identifiable addressee — and the most expensive to undo, because trophic cascades have no reverse button. Mao took two years to remove the sparrow from the list. Chile signed a thirty-year moratorium and wants to open it after eight and a half months, with a census that says «stable» and a bill title that says «population control». The blind spot is not ecological: it is that we are debating how many sea lions there are when the question was how much hake is left.

Atlas of the problem · 7 dimensions
Countries in the headline
Chile. The discussion is framed as a domestic problem between southern artisanal fishers and a protected species.
Countries in the actual story
China (1958-1962), the United States (Yellowstone 1926 and the Columbia River today), Namibia and South Africa (1990 onwards) and Australia (the dingo fence). Five prior runs of the same experiment, none of them cited in the bill.
Who profits
The political return on a visible measure with an identifiable addressee, and the industry that is spared a discussion of quotas, gear and extractive capacity for as long as the debate is about sea lions.
Who pays
The artisanal fishers themselves, once the food web reorders and the hake does not come back; the public budget of the control programme; and a population the national census describes as stable.
5 / 10-year horizon
In 5 years: interaction with nets continues, because the sea lion learns where the net is, not where the permit is. In 10: either extractive capacity is discussed, or the same argument reappears applied to another species.
Regulatory gap
A thirty-year moratorium in force since November 2025 against a bill that opens it after eight and a half months; and «adaptive management based on scientific evidence» with no published baseline later than the 2019 census.
Blind spot
Legislation follows what is visible. The sea lion is visible; decades of accumulated overfishing are not. The entire discussion happens on the visible variable and none of it on the causal one.

Detail on the papers from section 03.

Paper 01Helium escaping from LHS 1140 b

DisciplineAstronomy / Exoplanets
RegionU.S. (Harvard) · observed from Chile (Las Campanas)
PublicationScience · 16 July 2026
Summary
Detection of metastable helium escaping the upper atmosphere of LHS 1140 b, a super-Earth in the habitable zone of a red dwarf 48 light-years away. It is the first confirmed atmosphere on a habitable-zone rocky planet.
Methodology
High-resolution transit spectroscopy with the 6.5 m Magellan telescopes at Las Campanas Observatory, on a transit observed in September 2024, targeting the 1083 nm helium triplet signature.
Limitations
Helium is indirect evidence: it confirms an atmosphere exists, not its dominant composition or its habitability. No water or biologically relevant molecule was detected. The atmosphere could be a heavy remnant after billions of years of stellar erosion. «Habitable zone» is an orbital definition, not a biological one.

Paper 02Mitochondrial plaques in Alzheimer's disease

DisciplineNeuroscience / Neurodegeneration
RegionNorth America (University of Minnesota)
PublicationNature Neuroscience · 29 July 2026
Summary
Identification of «mitochondrial plaques», accumulations of mitochondria at different stages of degradation inside dystrophic neurites, resulting from mitophagy that begins but cannot complete owing to lysosomal dysfunction. They are described as a third pathological entity, distinct from amyloid plaques and tau tangles.
Methodology
Characterisation in two independent mouse models of the disease and validation in post-mortem human brain tissue, labelling neutral and acidic mitochondria to reconstruct degradation stages.
Limitations
The claim that mitochondrial plaques precede amyloid ones rests on inference across stages, not on longitudinal follow-up in humans, which post-mortem tissue cannot provide. Mouse models of Alzheimer's poorly reproduce human sporadic disease. Causality with respect to cognitive decline is not demonstrated.

nature.com · 10.1038/s41593-026-02390-1

Paper 03Direct hydrogen from mixed plastic waste

DisciplineChemistry / Chemical recycling
RegionNorth America (UCLA) · Asia (Ewha Womans University, South Korea)
PublicationPNAS · 6 July 2026
Summary
Selective and direct generation of high-purity hydrogen from a mixture of three mass-consumption plastics via alkaline thermal treatment, at temperatures markedly below conventional gasification, retaining carbon in solid mineral form rather than emitting it as CO₂.
Methodology
Alkaline thermal treatment applied to plastic mixtures without prior sorting, with characterisation of the purity of the hydrogen produced and of the carbon retained in the solid phase.
Limitations
Laboratory scale: there is no industrial-scale demonstration and no full energy balance for a process that still requires heat. Three polymers were tested, not the real heterogeneity of a municipal waste stream, which includes additives, fillers and contamination. The fate and long-term stability of the mineralised carbon require independent verification.
